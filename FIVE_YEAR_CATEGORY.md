## 2. Why the analogy game is a trap

Every entry above defines us as a derivative of something else. That is useful for a seed pitch and fatal as a five-year ambition.

**Duolingo is not "Rosetta Stone but an app."** It created *casual daily language practice* — a behaviour that did not previously exist. **Strava did not become "Nike Run Club but social."** It made *training data a form of identity*. In both cases the company outgrew its analogy by creating a category the analogy could not contain.

At ten million users we should not be *the something for something*. We should be the first company in a category people did not know existed.

## 3. The category: reasoning fitness

> **Agora created reasoning fitness — the idea that how well you think is trainable, measurable, and worth practising.**

This is the biggest available play, and the reason is a historical precedent that is easy to forget.

**"Physical fitness" is an invented category.** Before the 1960s, ordinary people did not train. Exercise was for athletes and soldiers. The notion that a middle-aged accountant should run for no competitive reason was strange. Then jogging, aerobics and gyms turned "fitness" into a normal thing that normal people did, and an enormous industry followed the idea rather than preceding it.

**Reasoning today sits exactly where fitness sat in 1960.** People believe it is a fixed trait — you are sharp or you are not. There is no cultural notion of *training* it, no accepted measure, no practice, no place to do it. The concept simply does not exist in ordinary life.

Creating that concept is a far larger prize than winning a market that already exists. It also makes the product obviously necessary rather than obviously optional: once someone accepts that thinking is trainable, the absence of a place to train it becomes conspicuous.

Three things must be true for the category to take:

| Requirement | Our answer |
|---|---|
| A **measure** people trust | The Reasoning Profile — honest, error-barred, falls as well as rises |
| A **practice** that is normal to do | The daily claim and the Gym — 90 seconds, unpretentious |
| A **social identity** that costs nothing to claim | *"I train my thinking"* — practice, not virtue |

The fitness industry needed the same three: bathroom scales and the mile time, jogging, and the acceptability of being seen running in public. We need all three, and the third is the one most likely to fail.

## 4. The positioning ladder

Different audiences at different times need different sentences. One line cannot do all of it.

**To a user, today:** *"One claim a day. Take a position before you see what anyone else thinks."*
Concrete, small, immediately understood, promises nothing grandiose.

**To a user, after a month:** *"The gym for judgment."*
By then they have felt the training and the metaphor pays off.

**To an institution:** *"Verified evidence of how someone reasons."*
Buyers of the credential care about the record, not the practice.

**To the world, at scale:** *"Agora made thinking trainable."*
The category claim, and the only one worth aiming at.

**Never:** *"Become a more rational person."* Superiority claim, repellent, and it recruits precisely the users we least want (`IDENTITY_DESIGN.md` §3).

## 5. What people actually say

You asked what people say about Agora at ten million users. The honest answer is that successful products are not described by their positioning; they are described by their **ritual**.

Nobody says "Wordle is a daily vocabulary puzzle." They say *"did you do today's?"*

So the realistic cultural footprint is not a tagline but a question people ask each other:

> **"What did you put for today's?"**

And, occasionally, the sentence I would most like to hear:

> *"I've been doing this thing where you commit to a position before you see what everyone else thinks. It's made me realise how often I was just agreeing with the room."*

That is a person describing a discovery about themselves, not a product. It is the strongest form of word of mouth there is, and it cannot be manufactured — only earned by the mechanic actually doing that to people.

## 6. Where the category could go wrong

**Perceived as elitist.** "Reasoning training" can read as a product for people who think they are smarter than you. Mitigation: the practice identity, plain language, no Latin, and mind-changing as the honoured act. This is the most likely failure and it is a matter of tone in ten thousand small copy decisions.

**Perceived as political.** A reasoning platform is an obvious target for the claim that it teaches one side's conclusions. Mitigation: the mirror test as a release gate, published symmetry results, and assigned-side debate as a visible, legible practice.

**Perceived as homework.** "Training" implies effort, and effort loses to entertainment. Mitigation: the 90-second floor, and the Daily as a puzzle rather than a lesson.

**Diluted into "AI writing help."** The commercially easiest drift, and the one that ends the category. Mitigation: the no-ghostwriting rule, held absolutely.

---

## 7. The one-line answer

If forced to a single sentence:

> **Agora is where you train how you think — the way a gym is where you train how you move.**

It names the practice, borrows a category people already accept, makes no claim about the user's intelligence, implies effort rather than talent, and leaves room to grow into the credential and the science.

And the ambition behind it, which is the real answer to your question:

> **In five years, "I train my reasoning" should sound as ordinary as "I go to the gym" — and Agora should be the reason.**

That is a category, not a product. Everything in the last five documents is in service of it.
