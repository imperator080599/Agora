# IDENTITY DESIGN — What Users Become

**Status:** Proposed.
**Date:** 2026-07-25

---

## 1. Why most of the candidate identities fail

You offered six directions. I want to reject five of them, and the reason they fail is the same reason, which is useful to name because it constrains everything after.

| Candidate | Verdict |
|---|---|
| *"I am a thinker"* | **Unsayable.** Nobody says this aloud without embarrassment. Fails the dinner-party test instantly. |
| *"I am a rational person"* | **Actively repellent.** It is a superiority claim, it is unfalsifiable, and it is already the badge of a particular online subculture people avoid being mistaken for. The worst option on the list. |
| *"I am someone who understands the world"* | **Arrogant**, and it invites immediate contradiction. |
| *"I am intellectually curious"* | **Undifferentiating.** Everyone believes this about themselves. It signals nothing. |
| *"I am a lifelong learner"* | **Dead language.** LinkedIn-speak, empty from overuse. |
| *"I am someone who can change minds"* | **The only viable one** — and the reason is instructive. |

### The rule underneath

> **Identity claims that assert *virtue* are socially costly. Identity claims that assert *practice* are socially safe.**

Look at what actually works:

- *"I'm a runner"* does not claim you are fast. It claims you run.
- *"I'm learning Spanish"* does not claim fluency. It claims you are trying.
- *"I play chess"* does not claim you are good.

Each is **humble, unfalsifiable, and repeatable**. Nobody can contradict you, nobody resents you for it, and you can say it again next year at any skill level.

Now apply the test: *"I'm a rational person"* claims a quality, invites contradiction, and makes you look like you think you're better than the listener. **It is the single most repellent sentence in the whole candidate set**, and any identity we build must be able to survive being said out loud to someone who disagrees with you politically.

Five of the six candidates fail because they describe *what you are*. The one that survives describes *what you do*.

## 2. The three-layer identity

One phrase cannot serve every context. Real identities have registers — what you say casually, what you say to someone who asks properly, and what you believe about yourself when nobody is listening.

### Layer 1 · The casual claim — *"I do the daily claim"*

What you say in passing, at work, in a group chat. Deliberately small, ritual-shaped, and status-free. **The Wordle register.**

Its power is precisely that it claims nothing. It is a *practice mentioned*, not a *virtue asserted*, which is what makes it repeatable in company without anyone rolling their eyes. This is also our organic growth surface: *"what was today's?"* is a question people ask each other, and it requires no evangelism.

### Layer 2 · The considered claim — *"I train my thinking."*

What you say when someone asks properly. The **Strava register**: the gym analogy is doing real work here because it is socially legible, humble, and implies effort rather than talent.

Reasoning is currently understood as something you *have*. This layer reframes it as something you *do*, and that reframe is the whole cultural bet (`FIVE_YEAR_CATEGORY.md`). Someone who says "I train my thinking" has already accepted a proposition most people have never considered — and once accepted, it makes the product obviously necessary rather than obviously optional.

### Layer 3 · The private identity — *"I'd rather be corrected than be wrong."*

What you believe about yourself. The deepest layer, and the one that produces years of retention.

It works where the others fail because it is a **preference, not a possession**. It claims no ability. It is disarming rather than superior — announcing that you are correctable is the opposite of announcing you are right. And it is genuinely, unusually rare: almost the entire internet is built on the reverse premise.

Its public expression is the one you already identified: *"I'm someone who can be talked out of things."* Counter-cultural, memorable, humble, admirable, and something a person could actually be proud of.

**The two layers reinforce.** "I train my thinking" explains the effort; "I'd rather be corrected" explains why the effort is worth it. Together they describe someone who is trying to be right rather than trying to look right — and that is a person most people would be glad to be.

## 3. The anti-identity — what we must prevent

Every product creates a stereotype of its user, and that stereotype determines who joins. Ours is at genuine risk of becoming:

> **The Well-Actually Guy.** Corrects people at parties. Names fallacies in Latin. Treats every conversation as a contest. Confuses being pedantic with being rigorous. Insufferable.

If that becomes the perceived Agora user, we lose everyone worth having and keep the people who least need us. This risk is real, it is the default outcome for products in this category, and it must be designed against structurally rather than discouraged in a code of conduct.

**Six structural defences:**

| Defence | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Mind-changing is the highest status act** | Not winning. The one behaviour we most visibly honour is being persuaded |
| **No public scores or rankings** | Nothing to be smug *with* |
| **The coach never uses Latin** | *"You've shown these happened in order, not that one caused the other"* — vocabulary that clarifies rather than credentials |
| **Steelmanning is a core practice** | Regularly arguing the other side is corrosive to contempt in a way no policy is |
| **Calibration is measured** | Overconfidence has a number, and it is your own |
| **Tone in every surface** | Curious, not combative. The product never celebrates a "win" |

**The tell we should watch for:** if users start posting screenshots of the coach criticising *other people's* arguments, the anti-identity is winning and we should intervene immediately.

## 4. Identity formation over time

Identity is built from evidence a person accumulates about themselves, not from labels we assign. Each stage needs a specific, undeniable moment.

| Stage | Belief | The evidence that produces it |
|---|---|---|
| **Week 1** | *"I have views and can say why."* | Published something; it did not go badly |
| **Month 1** | *"I can tell when an argument is doing something suspicious."* | Spotted a flaw unprompted, in the wild |
| **Month 3** | *"I catch my own mistakes."* | **Self-caught before the coach flagged it** |
| **Month 6** | *"I argue with the best version of the other side."* | Wrote a steelman an opponent endorsed |
| **Year 1** | *"This is how I think now."* | Used it at work, unprompted, and noticed |
| **Year 2** | *"I'd rather be corrected than be wrong."* | Changed their mind publicly, more than once, and it felt good rather than humiliating |

**The month-3 self-catch is the hinge.** Before it, a user is someone being coached. After it, they are someone who reasons carefully and occasionally gets help. That is a change in self-concept rather than in skill, and it is the strongest available predictor of whether someone is still here at month twelve.

We should detect it and name it, because the user may not notice:

> *"You hedged that claim before I said anything. Three months ago I'd have flagged it."*

**A note on public mind-changing.** Layer 3 only becomes real when someone changes their mind *where others can see* and finds it survivable — even good. That is why the mechanic must be socially rewarded rather than merely permitted. The first public reversal that goes well is the moment the identity locks in.

## 5. Belonging

Identity needs a group, and the group must be small.

Global membership does not confer identity — nobody's self-concept comes from being one of ten million users. **Identity forms in groups of eight to twelve**, where you are known, missed when absent, and have a reputation you would rather not damage. This is why Reddit's identity lives in subreddits and Strava's in clubs rather than in the global feed.

**The Circle** (`SOCIAL_LEARNING_DESIGN.md` §2) is the identity unit: a small persistent group who argue the same claims, know each other's tendencies, and hold each other to a standard.

The specific belonging we are offering is unusual and worth naming precisely: **not people who agree with you — people who will take your argument seriously enough to attack it properly.** That is rarer and more valuable than agreement, and almost nowhere else on the internet provides it.

## 6. What we put on the profile

The profile is the identity artifact, so it must express the identity above rather than contradict it.

**On it:**
- Earned titles the user chooses to display
- Public arguments, dated
- **Times they changed their mind** — displayed with pride, positioned as achievement
- Verified evidence contributed
- Circle membership
- Season count — *"training since 2026"*, the Strava-year move

**Never on it:**
- Any score, level, or rank
- Follower or view counts
- Persuasion events as a running total (meaningful individually, a popularity metric in aggregate)
- Win/loss records — there is nothing to win
- Faculty levels — those are the mirror, and mirrors are private

**The single most important line on the page** should be the count of times this person changed their mind under evidence. It is the one statistic that is simultaneously a genuine achievement, impossible to fake convincingly, unavailable on any other platform, and a complete inoculation against the Well-Actually Guy — because it advertises correctability rather than correctness.

## 7. The sentence we want people to say

Not our positioning line. The line a real user says to a friend, unprompted:

> *"I've been doing this thing where you take a position on a claim before you see what anyone else thinks. It's made me realise how often I was just agreeing with the room."*

Three properties make it the right target. It describes **a practice, not a virtue**. It reports **a discovery about themselves**, which is what people actually enjoy recounting. And it is **an admission of a former weakness**, which is disarming rather than superior — so the listener leans in rather than backing away.

If people say that sentence, we have built the right thing. If they say *"I'm a really rational person now"*, we have built the wrong one.
