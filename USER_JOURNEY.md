# USER JOURNEY — How a Person Becomes a Better Thinker

**Status:** Proposed. Companion to `VISION_V2.md` and `COMPOSER_EXPERIENCE.md`.
**Date:** 2026-07-25

---

## The person

**Maya, 29, product manager, London.** Reads the FT most mornings. Has opinions about the economy she has never had to defend. Once left a long comment on a news site, immediately regretted it, and never did it again. She is not looking for a debate app — she would tell you she finds online argument exhausting.

She is exactly who this is for, and the reason is in that last sentence: she avoids arguing online not because she lacks views but because every existing venue punishes her for having them badly. Our job is to build the first place where being imperfectly right is safe and productive.

The journey below is written for her. Where a stage has a failure mode, it is stated — because most of these stages *will* fail for most users, and designing as if they won't is how products die politely.

---

## DAY 1 — "I didn't know I thought that"

### 0:00 — Arrival

She lands on a single claim. No feed, no signup wall, no product tour.

> **The 4-day work week is a luxury of rich economies.**

Three sourced context bullets. Two short quotes, one from each side. Three buttons.

**No numbers anywhere.** She cannot tell which side is winning, which means she cannot outsource the decision.

### 0:20 — The commit

She taps **Agree**. It felt obvious.

This is the first thing the product teaches, and it teaches it before she has read a word of instruction: **you form your own view first.** Commit-before-reveal is not a growth mechanic. It is lesson one.

### 0:22 — The reveal

The bar wipes in. **83% agree.** She is with the majority.

Two seconds later, the margin note:

> *You're with the majority here. The interesting question is whether you'd hold this if you weren't.*

That single sentence is the product's whole personality: it does not congratulate her for being popular.

### 0:40 — The invitation

> **Why do you think that?**
> *Most people who agree with you haven't said why. Take two minutes.*

This is the highest-leverage moment in the entire funnel. She has just discovered she is in a majority she did not earn, and the invitation arrives precisely there.

**She declines.** Most people will, on day one, and the product does not sulk. It shows her the debate map — the strongest case for and against, properly attributed — and she reads it for ninety seconds.

**She leaves having learned something and having written nothing.** That is a successful day one.

> **Failure mode:** if declining the invitation feels like failing, she does not come back. The copy after a decline must be genuinely neutral — *"Fair enough. Here's what other people argued."* — with no re-prompt, no badge withheld, no guilt.

### 0:00 next morning — The hook

One notification, 08:00 local:

> *Today's claim is about chip subsidies. You have 12 hours to take a position.*

No streak pressure on day one. No "you're missing out."

**Day 1 success:** she took a position and read the debate. Nothing more.

## WEEK 1 — "Fine. I'll explain myself."

### Days 2–3: the habit forms

Two more claims, two more taps. Fifteen seconds each. She now has three positions in a ledger she has not looked at.

### Day 4: the crack

The claim is **"Remote work reduces innovation."** She disagrees strongly — she has managed a distributed team for three years and she has *opinions*.

The reveal shows **62% agree**. She is in a minority, and it is a minority she believes is wrong.

> **Why do you think that?**
> *You're in the minority here. That usually means you know something the majority doesn't.*

**She taps it.** Not because the product nagged, but because being outnumbered on something she has lived is uncomfortable in a productive way. Minority status is the strongest natural trigger for wanting to explain yourself, and the product should route composition invitations toward it.

### Day 4, +90 seconds: the first composition

The Composer opens. Paper, a serif, a caret, and a margin that says *"I'll read as you write."*

**Idea.** She types: *"Remote work doesn't reduce innovation, bad management does."*

> `affirm` — *That's a position — it says something the claim doesn't. Now the mechanism.*

**Argument.** She writes three sentences about distributed teams and asynchronous decision-making. Then:

> `flag` — *"Obviously" is doing a lot of work here. One counterexample sinks it. Would a narrower claim still support your idea?*

She stares at that for four seconds, deletes "obviously", and rewrites the sentence more carefully. **That four seconds is the product working.** Nobody has ever pointed at her reasoning before.

Then:

> `counter` — *A reader will say: breakthroughs come from unplanned collisions that scheduling software can't reproduce. What's your answer?* → `[ Address this ]`

She taps it. A blank paragraph appears with a ghosted prompt. She writes the rebuttal herself — and it is the best paragraph in her argument, because it is the only one she wrote against resistance.

**Evidence.** Three cards are already waiting. She picks the GitLab Remote Report; it flies into her argument as a citation.

**Publish.** Total elapsed: 2 minutes 40 seconds.

### Day 6: the thing that changes everything

A notification:

> **Your argument moved someone.**
> *A reader changed from Agree to It's complicated after reading your reasoning on remote work.*

She reads it twice.

**This is the moment Agora becomes different from every other product she uses.** Not because the notification is clever — because a stranger's mind moved, measurably, because of words she wrote. No chatbot can manufacture this. It is the whole moat in one push notification, and it arrives on day six.

> **Failure mode:** if she publishes and hears nothing back, week one is a dead end. Persuasion events depend on readers, which is why the tap path must stay enormous. **We should guarantee a first-week response** — seeding early cohorts so no first argument goes unread is a legitimate and honest intervention.

**Week 1 success:** one argument published, one persuasion event received.

## MONTH 1 — "I can see myself changing"

### The rhythm

Roughly: positions on most days, arguments on two or three claims a week. She has stopped noticing the tap and started anticipating the reveal.

### What the coach starts doing differently

The margin has begun to reference her own history:

> `flag` — *This is the third time you've moved from correlation to cause on an economics claim. It's your most common pattern.*

**No cold chatbot can say this**, and it is the first genuine payoff of the longitudinal reasoning record. It is also the moment personalisation stops being a feature and becomes a reason to stay: her coach knows her, and starting over elsewhere means starting over as a stranger.

### The first mind change

Day 23. She takes a position, reads the debate map, and finds an argument that is simply better than hers. She changes her position from Agree to It's complicated.

The product's response matters enormously here:

> *You changed your mind. That's the hardest thing this product asks of anyone.*

It is recorded in her ledger as an achievement, never as an inconsistency. **Making mind-changing the highest-status act on the platform is the mechanical antidote to the smugness that kills every product in this category.**

### The ledger becomes legible

She opens it properly for the first time. Twenty-eight positions, six arguments, four persuasion events, two mind-changes. In mono type, dated, like a record.

At the top, one line:

> *You've earned more persuasion events per argument this month than last. Your arguments are landing.*

### Month 1 success

- Positions on 60%+ of days
- 4–8 arguments published
- At least one mind change
- Opened her ledger unprompted

## MONTH 6 — "This changed how I argue at work"

### Measurable improvement

Blind comparison of her month-1 and month-6 arguments (`VISION_V2.md` §11) shows:

| | Month 1 | Month 6 |
|---|---|---|
| Flagged reasoning problems per 100 words | 3.2 | 0.9 |
| Arguments carrying evidence | 41% | 88% |
| Arguments engaging the opposing side | 18% | 71% |
| Persuasion events per argument | 0.4 | 1.9 |
| **Coach interventions needed per argument** | **4.1** | **1.3** |

That final row is the one that matters. **The coach has become less necessary.** She now anticipates the counterargument before the margin raises it — she has internalised the move. That is what teaching looks like when it works, and it is the only metric that cannot be faked by a more talkative assistant.

### The transfer

The real evidence is outside the product. In a planning meeting she says *"what's the strongest version of the argument against this?"* — a habit the Composer drilled into her over six months. Nobody at that table knows where she learned it.

**Transfer is the honest goal.** A user who reasons better only inside Agora has learned a UI, not a skill.

### What keeps her

Not streaks. Three things:

1. **The record** — six months of her own thinking, and the ability to see it change. Costly to abandon and impossible to export elsewhere.
2. **The coach knows her** — her patterns, her blind spots, the specific way she over-reaches.
3. **The people** — a handful of pseudonymous regulars whose arguments she respects and who have occasionally changed her mind. Not friends. Worthy opponents, which is rarer.

### Month 6 success

- Reasoning improvement demonstrable under blind evaluation
- Coach dependency down, not up
- She has changed her mind at least three times
- She has told someone about it

## The stage-gate view

| Stage | The user's question | What must be true | Primary failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Day 1** | Is this interesting? | One claim, no numbers, no signup, no tour | Feels like a survey |
| **Week 1** | Is it worth my effort? | Minority-position trigger + a persuasion event | Publishes into silence |
| **Month 1** | Am I getting anything back? | Coach references her history; ledger becomes legible | Coach feels generic |
| **Month 6** | Has this changed me? | Measurable improvement; declining coach dependency | Improvement is illusory (§11 circularity) |

---

## What we owe the 90% who never write

Most users will never publish an argument. They are **not** a funnel leak. They are the audience, the opponents, and the source of every persuasion event a writer receives.

They must get real value:

- The daily claim as a genuine two-minute thinking habit
- The debate map as high-quality curated argument on both sides
- Their own ledger — a record of how *their* views moved, which is interesting without ever writing a word
- The `MOVED ME` action, which requires no writing and is the mechanism that produces persuasion events for others

A tap-only user who changes their mind three times in six months has become a better thinker by any honest definition. **The product succeeds for them too, and the copy should never imply otherwise.**
